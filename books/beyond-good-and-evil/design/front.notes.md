## 0. What the five chapters actually say (reading record)

### p1c1 — Preface (6 ¶¶, 696 words)
- ¶1: "SUPPOSING that Truth is a woman—what then?" Dogmatists' "terrible
  seriousness and clumsy importunity" as failed courtship. **Truth is a woman
  who refuses to be won.**
- ¶2: "every kind of dogma stands with sad and discouraged mien… it has
  fallen, all dogma lies on the ground—nay more, it is at its last gasp."
  Dogmatizing as "a noble puerilism," built on "some popular superstition of
  immemorial time," "some play upon words, a deception on the part of grammar."
  **Fallen architecture; rubble of edifices.**
- ¶3: dogmatism "was only a promise," as astrology once was — "we owe to it…
  the grand style of architecture." **Stars, Asian/Egyptian monumentality.**
- ¶4: great things "wander about the earth as enormous and awe-inspiring
  caricatures" — Vedanta in Asia, Platonism in Europe. "Plato's invention of
  Pure Spirit and the Good in Itself" is "the worst, the most tiresome, and
  the most dangerous of errors." **Caricature / mask foretaste.**
- ¶5: "Europe, rid of this nightmare, can again draw breath… we, WHOSE DUTY
  IS WAKEFULNESS ITSELF." "CHRISTIANITY IS PLATONISM FOR THE 'PEOPLE'."
  "a magnificent tension of soul… with such a tensely strained bow one can
  now aim at the furthest goals." Jesuitism and democratic enlightenment as
  two attempts "to unbend the bow." "we GOOD EUROPEANS, and free, VERY free
  spirits—we have it still, all the distress of spirit and all the tension
  of its bow! And perhaps also the arrow, the duty, and, who knows? THE GOAL
  TO AIM AT…" **The strained bow is the chapter's load-bearing image.**
- ¶6: "Sils Maria Upper Engadine, JUNE, 1885." **Alpine dateline; high-air
  provenance.**

### p1c2 — Prejudices of Philosophers (§1–23, 75 ¶¶, 7430 words)
- §1 (¶1–2): "The Will to Truth… what strange, perplexing, questionable
  questions!" "That this Sphinx teaches us at last to ask questions
  ourselves? WHO is it… WHAT really is this 'Will to Truth' in us?"
  "WHY NOT RATHER untruth? And uncertainty? Even ignorance?"
  "Which of us is the Oedipus here? Which the Sphinx?… a rendezvous of
  questions and notes of interrogation." **Sphinx vs Oedipus; interrogation
  marks as visual matter.**
- §2 (¶3–4): "HOW COULD anything originate out of its opposite?" "The
  fundamental belief of metaphysicians is THE BELIEF IN ANTITHESES OF
  VALUES." "frog perspectives… to borrow an expression current among
  painters." "philosophers of the dangerous 'Perhaps'." **Antithesis pairs;
  the frog's-eye view (low, squashed perspective).**
- §4 (¶8–9): "The falseness of an opinion is not for us any objection to
  it." "TO RECOGNISE UNTRUTH AS A CONDITION OF LIFE… has thereby alone
  placed itself beyond good and evil." **Title-drop; untruth as foundation.**
- §5 (¶10–12): philosophers as advocates; "the Tartuffery of old Kant… with
  which he entices us into the dialectic by-ways"; Spinoza's "hocus-pocus in
  mathematical form… clad his philosophy in mail and mask"; "that invincible
  maiden, that Pallas Athene." **Masks, mail, Tartuffe-theatricality.**
- §6 (¶13–15): every great philosophy "the confession of its originator, and
  a species of involuntary and unconscious auto-biography"; impulses as
  "INSPIRING GENII (or as demons and cobolds)"; the scholar's "small,
  independent clock-work." **Demons/cobolds; clockwork mechanism.**
- §7 (¶16–19): Epicurus' "Dionysiokolakes… 'They are all ACTORS'"; Plato's
  "grandiose manner, the mise en scene style." **Theatre again: actors,
  staging.**
- §8 (¶20–22): conviction enters "as an ancient mystery: Adventavit asinus,
  Pulcher et fortissimus." **The ass walks on stage — comic procession.**
- §9 (¶23–24): Nature as "boundlessly extravagant, boundlessly indifferent…
  imagine to yourselves INDIFFERENCE as a power"; "the most spiritual Will to
  Power, the will to 'creation of the world.'" **Indifferent cosmos.**
- §11 (¶28–35): Kant "proud of his Table of Categories"; "BY MEANS OF A
  MEANS (faculty)"; "the honeymoon of German philosophy… went immediately
  into the groves—all seeking for 'faculties'"; Molière's "virtus
  dormitiva… Quia est in eo virtus dormitiva, Cujus est natura sensus
  assoupire." **Shatterable stone tables; soporific Latin comedy.**
- §12 (¶36–38): Boscovich/Copernicus "the greatest and most successful
  opponents of ocular evidence"; "relentless war to the knife" on
  "SOUL-ATOMISM… as a monad, as an atomon." **War on the atom; anti-ocular.**
- §13 (¶39–40): "life itself is WILL TO POWER."
- §14 (¶41): "pale, cold, grey conceptional networks which they threw over
  the motley whirl of the senses." **Grey nets over motley whirls — a
  drawable texture if ever there was one.**
- §17 (¶50–52): "a thought comes when 'it' wishes, and not when 'I' wish";
  grammar's "To think is an activity; every activity requires an agency."
- §19 (¶56–59): will as plurality; "our body is but a social structure
  composed of many souls"; "L'EFFET C'EST MOI." **Many souls, one name.**
- §20 (¶61–62): ideas "grow up in connection… as the collective members of
  the fauna of a Continent"; "they always revolve once more in the same
  orbit"; "the common philosophy of grammar"; "philosophizing is so far a
  kind of atavism." **Orbits, fauna-systems, return.**
- §21 (¶63–65): "CAUSA SUI… with more than Munchausen daring, to pull
  oneself up into existence by the hair, out of the slough of nothingness."
  **Munchausen self-hauling — comic-engraving gold.**
- §22 (¶69–72): "'Ni dieu, ni maitre'… 'Cheers for natural law!'"; "every
  power effects its ultimate consequences every moment."
- §23 (¶73–75): "psychology as the Morphology and DEVELOPMENT-DOCTRINE OF
  THE WILL TO POWER"; "we crush out, we destroy perhaps the remains of our
  own morality by daring to make our voyage thither"; "psychology shall once
  more be recognized as the queen of the sciences." **Sea-voyage past
  morality; psychology crowned queen.**

### p1c3 — The Free Spirit (§24–44, 59 ¶¶, 6684 words)
- §24 (¶1): "O sancta simplicitas!"; "this SIMPLIFIED, thoroughly artificial,
  suitably imagined, and suitably falsified world"; "the will to ignorance,
  to the uncertain, to the untrue! Not as its opposite, but—as its
  refinement!" **Simplification as granite foundation.**
- §25 (¶2–4): "beware of martyrdom!"; "ye knights of the sorrowful
  countenance"; "have your masks and your ruses"; "don't forget the garden,
  the garden with golden trellis-work!"; "Choose the GOOD solitude, the
  free, wanton, lightsome solitude"; philosophers deteriorated into
  "stage-and-tribune-bawlers… merely a satyric play." **Masks, golden
  trellis garden, lightsome solitude vs. the stage.**
- §26 (¶5–7): "Every select man strives instinctively for a citadel and a
  privacy"; "he would go 'inside'"; cynics who "wallow, even in books, as on
  their own dung-hill"; "a scientific head… placed on an ape's body"; "no
  one is such a LIAR as the indignant man." **Citadel; ape + scientific
  head.**
- §27 (¶8–10): thinking "gangasrotogati [like the river Ganges: presto]"
  among those who live "kurmagati [like the tortoise: lento]" or
  "froglike, mandeikagati [staccato]." "I do everything to be 'difficultly
  understood' myself!" **Three tempos: river-presto, tortoise-lento,
  frog-staccato. A typographic-tempo idea.**
- §28 (¶11–16): "What is most difficult to render… is the TEMPO of its
  style"; "A German is almost incapacitated for PRESTO"; Machiavelli's
  "boisterous allegrissimo"; Petronius with "the feet of a wind";
  Aristophanes the "transfiguring, complementary genius"; Plato's
  death-pillow held "no 'Bible'… but a book of Aristophanes." **Presto vs.
  ponderous; wind-feet; the comedian under the pillow.**
- §29 (¶17): independence: "He enters into a labyrinth… torn piecemeal by
  some minotaur of conscience." **Labyrinth + minotaur.**
- §30 (¶18–20): "the esoteric class views things FROM ABOVE DOWNWARDS";
  "heights of the soul from which tragedy itself no longer appears to
  operate tragically"; "One should not go into churches if one wishes to
  breathe PURE air." **Altitude; pure air above tragedy.**
- §32 (¶23–25): pre-moral → moral → "ULTRA-MORAL"; "the intention is only a
  sign or symptom"; morality as "astrology and alchemy… something which must
  be surmounted." **Three ages; threshold.**
- §34 (¶27–33): "the ERRONEOUSNESS of the world"; "different valeurs, as the
  painters say"; "Why might not the world WHICH CONCERNS US—be a fiction?";
  "philosophy should renounce governess-faith." **Painter's valeurs
  (value-steps) — a tonal-scale motif.**
- §36 (¶35–43): "The world seen from within… would simply be 'Will to
  Power,' and nothing else."
- §39 (¶46–48): "the strength of a mind might be measured by the amount of
  'truth' it could endure"; Stendhal: "il faut etre sec, clair, sans
  illusion." **Dry, clear, without illusion — the chapter's style law.**
- §40 (¶49–50): "Everything that is profound loves the mask"; "a man with
  something costly and fragile to conceal, would roll through life… like an
  old, green, heavily-hooped wine-cask"; "around every profound spirit there
  continually grows a mask." **THE mask chapter. Hooped cask; growing mask.**
- §41 (¶51): "Not to cleave to any person… every person is a prison and also
  a recess"; "the voluptuous distance and remoteness of the bird, which
  always flies further aloft"; "the danger of the flier." **The high bird;
  non-cleaving.**
- §42–44 (¶52–59): coming philosophers as "tempters"; "'My opinion is MY
  opinion'"; "the great things remain for the great, the abysses for the
  profound"; "free, VERY free spirits"; levellers' "universal,
  green-meadow happiness of the herd"; "Beyond Good and Evil" as "the
  dangerous formula"; "the born, sworn, jealous friends of SOLITUDE, of our
  own profoundest midnight and midday solitude." **Tempters; midnight AND
  midday solitude (two solitudes, two lights).**

### p1c4 — The Religious Mood (§45–62, 46 ¶¶, 5506 words)
- §45 (¶1–3): soul as "virgin forest" and "big hunt"; Pascal's "intellectual
  conscience"; "the love of truth has its reward in heaven."
  **Forest-hunt; hounds.**
- §46 (¶4–5): Pascal's faith as "a continuous suicide of reason—a tough,
  long-lived, worm-like reason"; "sacrifice… subjection, self-derision, and
  self-mutilation"; "'God on the Cross'… a transvaluation of all ancient
  values"; "the Oriental slave who thus took revenge on Rome."
  **Suicide of reason; cross as inversion engine; Orient vs Rome.**
- §47 (¶6–8): "solitude, fasting, and sexual abstinence"; "penitential
  paroxysms, world-renunciation, and will-renunciation"; "How is the
  negation of will POSSIBLE? how is the saint possible?"; Kundry; "the
  religious neurosis… made its latest epidemical outbreak… as the 'Salvation
  Army'"; "What? 'Miracle' only an error of interpretation? A lack of
  philology?" **Saint as miracle-wreck of psychology.**
- §48 (¶9–12): North vs South faith; "LA NIAISERIE RELIGIEUSE PAR
  EXCELLENCE!" **Marginal rage-note — the annotator's hand.**
- §49 (¶13): Greek religion's "irrestrainable stream of GRATITUDE";
  "when the populace got the upper hand… FEAR became rampant also in
  religion." **Gratitude-stream → fear-flood.**
- §50 (¶14): "The passion for God" — Luther's churlish kind, Augustine's
  "Oriental exaltation," Guyon's "UNIO MYSTICA ET PHYSICA." **Three
  temperatures of God-passion.**
- §51 (¶15–16): the mighty bow before the saint's "self-subjugation and
  utter voluntary privation"; "it was the 'Will to Power' which obliged them
  to halt before the saint." **The saint halting kings.**
- §52 (¶17–18): Old Testament's "immense scale"; New Testament as "a kind of
  ROCOCO of taste"; binding both as one book "the greatest audacity and
  'sin against the Spirit'." **Colossi vs rococo.**
- §53 (¶19): "'The father' in God is thoroughly refuted"; God "seems
  incapable of communicating himself clearly." **Unreachable father.**
- §55 (¶25–27): "a great ladder of religious cruelty, with many rounds; but
  three of these are the most important": (1) human sacrifice, "the
  firstling sacrifices," Tiberius in the Mithra-Grotto; (2) sacrifice of
  "the strongest instincts… 'nature'"; (3) "to sacrifice God himself, and
  out of cruelty to themselves to worship stone, stupidity, gravity, fate,
  nothingness? To sacrifice God for nothingness." **THE ladder — the
  chapter's plate, with three oxblood rungs.**
- §56 (¶28): "circulus vitiosus deus?"; eternal "da capo." **Vicious circle
  as god.**
- §57 (¶29–30): "new stars, new enigmas, and notions are ever coming into
  view"; "'God' and 'sin' will one day seem… a child's plaything."
  **New stars; outgrown toys.**
- §58 (¶31–36): "outward idleness, or semi-idleness, is necessary to a real
  religious life… soft placidity called 'prayer'"; modern "noisy,
  time-engrossing… laboriousness educates and prepares for 'unbelief'."
  **Idleness vs the noisy factory of unbelief.**
- §59 (¶37–39): "men are superficial… flighty, lightsome, and false";
  "homines religiosi among the artists, as their HIGHEST rank"; "Piety…
  the most elaborate and ultimate product of the FEAR of truth";
  "man can become so artful, so superficial, so iridescent, and so good."
  **Iridescence; piety as beautification.**
- §60 (¶40–41): "To love mankind FOR GOD'S SAKE"; "flown highest and gone
  astray in the finest fashion." **Highest flight, finest straying.**
- §62 (¶44–46): "To REVERSE all estimates of value"; "a SUBLIME ABORTION
  of man"; "some divine hammer in his hand"; Pascal again as exhibit;
  "a dwarfed, almost ludicrous species… a gregarious animal." **The divine
  hammer; the sublime abortion.**

### p1c5 — Apophthegms and Interludes (§63–185, 129 ¶¶, 2540 words)
Form: 100+ numbered maxims, mostly 1–3 lines; titled sub-poems in caps
("THE SAGE AS ASTRONOMER," "FETTERED HEART, FREE SPIRIT," "ADVICE AS A

- §68: "'I did that,' says my memory. 'I could not have done that,' says my
  pride… Eventually—the memory yields." **Memory vs pride dialogue.**
- §71: "So long as thou feelest the stars as an 'above thee,' thou lackest
  the eye of the discerning one." **Stars-above; the discerning eye.**
- §81: "Is it necessary that you should so salt your truth that it will no
  longer—quench thirst?" **Salted truth; thirst.**
- §87: "FETTERED HEART, FREE SPIRIT." **Fetter + liberty in one line.**
- §91: "So cold, so icy, that one burns one's finger at the touch of him!"
  **Ice that burns.**
- §94: "the seriousness that one had as a child at play." **Play-serious.**
- §124: "He who exults at the stake… A parable." **The stake.**
- §146: "He who fights with monsters should be careful lest he thereby
  become a monster. And if thou gaze long into an abyss, the abyss will also
  gaze into thee." **The abyss-gaze — the most famous sentence in the book;
  the chapter's plate.**
- §150: "Around the hero everything becomes a tragedy; around the demigod
  everything becomes a satyr-play; and around God everything becomes—what?
  perhaps a 'world'?" **Three atmospheres.**
- §152: "'Where there is the tree of knowledge, there is always Paradise':
  so say the most ancient and the most modern serpents." **Serpents.**
- §153: "What is done out of love always takes place beyond good and evil."

- §154: "Objection, evasion, joyous distrust, and love of irony are signs of

- §156: "Insanity in individuals is something rare—but in groups, parties,

- §157: "The thought of suicide is a great consolation: by means of it one

- §164: "love God as I love him, as his Son! What have we Sons of God to do

- §168: "Christianity gave Eros poison to drink; he did not die of it,
  certainly, but degenerated to Vice." **Poisoned Eros.**
- §171: "Pity has an almost ludicrous effect on a man of knowledge, like
  tender hands on a Cyclops." **Hands on a Cyclops — grotesque tenderness.**

## 1. References studied (with citations)

1. **Henry van de Velde, Nietzsche-Archiv interiors, Villa Silberblick,
   Weimar (1902–03).** Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche's commission: porch,
   vestibule, library, study, dining room — total design down to stoves,
   lamps, floor tiles, vases. Lesson: a Nietzsche interior is
   *dark wood, lamplight warmth, whiplash ornament held under severe
   discipline* — density at the frame, calm at the reading surface. Our
   plates take the "framed dedication room" idea: each chapter opener a
   small dark chamber with one lit object. Source: Web Gallery of Art,
   "Nietzsche Archive: entrance hall by VELDE, Henry van de"
   (<https://www.wga.hu/html/v/velde3/13nietz1.html>); Klassik Stiftung
   Weimar Nietzsche-Archiv pages.
2. **Edvard Munch, _Friedrich Nietzsche_ (1906, oil, Thielska Galleriet,
   Stockholm).** Posthumous monumental portrait commissioned by Ernest
   Thiel, who held Munch to be "the foremost artistic interpreter of the
   philosopher's spirit." Lesson: the Nietzsche palette in paint is
   *bone-pale head emerging from near-black green-brown darks, with one
   hot accent* — brooding mass, not violet neon. Confirms bone-black +
   bone-pale + single blood-warm accent. Source: Thielska Galleriet,
   "Munch! — Nietzsche, Thiel and Scandinavia's greatest artist"
   (<https://www.thielskagalleriet.se/en/utstallning/munch/>).
3. **First edition: _Jenseits von Gut und Böse_, C. G. Naumann, Leipzig
   1886, set in Fraktur.** The book's first material form: dense blackletter
   page, aphorism numbers as furniture, thin rules, no illustration —
   authority from type alone. Lesson: display type must do heavy lifting;
   the number (§) is period furniture, not SaaS numbering; restraint of the
   text block is historically grounded. We do NOT revive Fraktur for body
   (illegible, wrong signal) but let one engraved display face carry
   "1886" while the body stays calm. Source: Wikimedia Commons first-edition
   scan ("Jenseits von Gut und Böse - 1886.jpg"); Christie's lot notes for
   the Naumann 1886 printing.
4. **Aubrey Beardsley, _Salomé_ illustrations (line block, 1894).**
   V&A/Met/LACMA holdings ("The Peacock Skirt," "The Black Cape," "The
   Dancer's Reward"). Lesson for our SVG medium: Beardsley proves *pure
   black-and-white line* — solid blacks, unmodulated contour, dotted
   stipple fields, vast empty paper — can carry decadence, cruelty, and
   erotic menace without any gradient. Our engraved plates adopt his
   economy: contour + hatch + stipple + void, one oxblood accent per plate
   maximum. Sources: V&A Explore the Collections O901555/O1026353/O1045835;
   Met collection 345737.

Synthesis for THIS project: dark warm chamber (Van de Velde) + bone-from-dark
portrait light (Munch) + type authority with numbered furniture (1886) +
black-line cruelty-with-restraint (Beardsley) = an engraved-plate book, not a
gradient book. Adopt: framed plates, bone/black massing, § furniture,
stipple-hatch texture. Avoid: neon accent on near-black, hero-gradient washes,
rounded-card grids, numbered "01/02/03" SaaS markers (our numbers are §
aphorism furniture — a different, older thing).

## 2. Three cohort directions (comparison)

### Direction A — "Weimar Engraving" (CHOSEN)
Midnight letterpress chamber. Bone-black ground, bone-pale type, oxblood +
brass accents. Each chapter opens with an original line-engraving-style SVG
plate (contour, cross-hatch, stipple) treating that chapter's central image
from its own sentences; calm Source Serif body below. Display in Fraunces at
high optical size. § numerals as brass furniture.
- Belongs to BGE because: the book IS a numbered-engraving sequence
  (§1–185 across the cohort); cruelty, masks, ladders, bows, abysses are
  burin subjects; 1886 blackletter authority translates to engraved serif
  authority; Nietzsche's hammer ("some divine hammer," §62) is literally an
  engraver's tool family.

### Direction B — "Sils-Maria Night" (rejected)
Deep Engadine-night blues, silver star charts, thin grotesque type; preface
astrology ("as was astrology… the grand style") as master motif; chapters as
constellation plates. Beautiful, but: the astrology passage is one paragraph
of one chapter; a blue monochrome is the rejected violet-storm's close cousin
and would read as "storm theme, bluer"; it flatters p1c1/p1c3's altitude and
starves p1c4's blood-warmth and p1c5's steel sparks. Title-swap test: works
equally well for any alpine book (Mann, Hesse) — fails the belonging test.

### Direction C — "Lapidary Rome" (rejected)
Travertine + imperial red, inscriptional capitals (Cinzel), chapters as
carved stone tablets; Kant's Table of Categories and §46's Rome-vs-Orient as
anchors. Strong for p1c2's tablets and p1c4's Roman passages, but: all-caps
inscriptional display kills the aphorist's presto speed (§27–28 demand
*tempo*, stone is lento); masks, ice, birds, abysses don't carve well;
reads "classics textbook" and would suit Marcus Aurelius better than
Nietzsche. Swap test: re-title it *Meditations* and nothing feels wrong —
fails.

### Why A belongs to THIS book (swap test)
Put *Meditations* or *The Brothers Karamazov* into Direction A and the plates
protest immediately: a tensely strained bow aimed past fallen ΔΟΓΜΑ blocks, a
Sphinx coiled on Kant's shattered Table, a hooped wine-cask mask, a
three-rung cruelty ladder ascending to an empty cloud, an abyss-eye ringed
with §-numeral sparks — every plate quotes a BGE sentence. Conversely, strip
the title off direction B or C and any book fits. A is also the only direction
whose medium (engraving: incision, pressure, the hammer-adjacent burin) puns
on the book's own method — "declare war, relentless war to the knife" (§12),
"some divine hammer in his hand" (§62).

## 3. Chosen direction — token system ("Weimar Engraving")

### Color (dark-first; 6 named values)
| Token | Hex | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Bone-Black | `#0D0B09` | page ground (warm, NOT violet-tinted) |
| Plate | `#171310` | elevated chamber / opener ground, TOC row hover |
| Bone Paper | `#EDE2CC` | display + body text, hatch-paper in plates |
| Printer's Ash | `#9A8F7A` | muted text, secondary hatch lines |
| Oxblood | `#93302E` | primary accent: cruelty, sacrifice, drop caps, p1c4's rungs, selection |
| Brass | `#C6A13B` | secondary accent: § numerals, Sphinx gold, rules, caption lines |

Light-theme behavior (keep legible, same grammar): ground → Bone Paper
`#EDE2CC`, text → Bone-Black, Oxblood deepens to `#7A2624`, Brass deepens to
`#8A6D1F`, hatch lines invert. Plates are authored as `currentColor`-aware
linework with fixed oxblood/brass spot layers so they survive all 8 themes;
sepia/light use the deepened spots. Never a violet or acid accent anywhere.

### Type (two families, clearly distinct roles)
- **Display: Fraunces** (Google Fonts, opsz 9–144, wght 300–900; SOFT/WONK
  axes available). Roles: chapter title, roman numeral, opener epigraph,
  plate captions (small caps-ish 12–13px tracked wide). Why: old-style bones
  with engraved poster presence; its "wonk" can give the preface bow-string
  tension without novelty-type illegibility. Fallback: Georgia, 'Times New
  Roman', serif.
- **Body: Source Serif 4** (Google Fonts, 400/400-italic/600). Roles: all
  reading paragraphs, TOC names. Why: calm, high x-height, sturdy at small
  sizes — Cormorant (current) is too light/faint for 7k-word aphorism
  chapters. Fallback: Georgia, serif.
- Scale (Elements of Typographic Style discipline): body 1.0625rem/1.75;
  chapter title clamp(2.6rem, 7vw, 4.6rem) Fraunces 560, tight leading 1.02;
  epigraph 1.15rem italic; § furniture 0.8125rem. Measure ≤ 42rem (~68ch in
  Source Serif). Reader chrome stays in the existing sans per shared
  contract — not our call.
- Tempo rule (from §27–28): p1c5 maxims may set 0.25rem tighter leading and
  slightly smaller size for presto; p1c2's long demolition §§ keep full
  leading (lento where the argument is heavy). Never letterspaced all-caps
  eyebrows; never single-word accent coloring in titles.

### Layout concept
```
┌─────────────────────────────────┐
│  PLATE (engraved SVG, framed,   │
│  dark chamber w/ caption rule)  │  ← one lit object per chapter
├─────────────────────────────────┤
│  ROMAN (ghost, Fraunces 144)    │
│  Title (Fraunces, 2 lines max)  │
│  — epigraph, one quoted line —  │  ← chapter's own sentence
├─────────────────────────────────┤
│  calm body column, 42rem,       │
│  drop cap ¶1, brass § furniture │
│  (where reader markup allows),  │
│  outer-margin plate vignette    │
│  watermark, very faint          │
└─────────────────────────────────┘
```
Left-aligned throughout; centered only the ghost roman. One orchestrated
moment per opener: the plate fades //draws in once (line-draw on first
paint, static after; disabled under reduced motion). No scroll-jacking,
no per-paragraph entrances.

### Principles (what makes it unlike anyone else)
1. **One lit object per chapter.** Each opener is a dark chamber with a
   single engraved plate — never a gradient, never a card row.
2. **Every plate quotes a sentence.** Caption under each plate is a verbatim
   fragment with its § reference. No decorative plates.
3. **Cruelty in line, kindness in type.** The art may be severe (Beardsley
   rule); the reading column is always calm, warm, and large.
4. **Numbers are furniture, not decoration.** § numerals, ghost romans, and
   plate numbers behave like 1886 page furniture.
5. **Oxblood is rationed.** One oxblood decision per chapter maximum (a rung,
   a drop cap, a seal) — blood means something or nothing.

## 4. Per-chapter treatments

### p1c1 — Preface · "The Strained Bow"
- **Rationale.** Six paragraphs, three images: Truth-as-woman who "has never
  allowed herself to be won" (¶1–2), fallen dogma "at its last gasp" amid
  "imposing and absolute philosophical edifices" (¶2), and the closing
  "tensely strained bow" with "perhaps also the arrow, the duty, and, who
  knows? THE GOAL TO AIM AT" (¶5), signed from "Sils Maria Upper Engadine,
  JUNE, 1885" (¶6). The treatment stages courtship-failure → rubble →
  tension: the whole book is the drawn bow.
- **Palette role.** Darkest, most nocturnal opener: Bone-Black ground, Bone
  linework, Brass single accent (the arrowhead + stars). No oxblood yet —
  blood enters with cruelty in p1c4. Establishes the chamber.
- **Motif / SVG concept** (`art/front-preface-bow.svg`, ~1600×900):
  foreground, a great recurve bow drawn to full tension, string a single
  unbroken vertical line, nocked arrow aimed up-right past the frame toward
  three brass stars; midground, fallen pediment blocks and a broken column
  drum, one block face incised ΔΟΓΜΑ, half-buried; background, stippled
  night + thin Engadine ridgeline; a small distant female silhouette walking
  away along the ridge (Truth unwon — keep tiny, contour only, no face).
  Technique: unmodulated contour + parallel hatch on the bow limbs +
  stipple sky. Caption: "with such a tensely strained bow one can now aim
  at the furthest goals. —Pref."
- **Opener composition.** Plate full-bleed at top in a thin brass keyline
  frame; ghost "0"/"Preface" roman; title "Preface" in Fraunces 560 with
  subtitle "Sils Maria Upper Engadine, June 1885" set as dateline furniture
  (not an eyebrow — it is the author's own signature line); epigraph:
  "SUPPOSING that Truth is a woman—what then?"
- **Reading treatment.** 696 words: intimate. Slightly larger body
  (1.125rem), drop cap "S" in Brass; final dateline paragraph (¶6) set
  right-aligned italic as a signature; generous after-space. Margin
  vignette: tiny bow-and-arrow mark, Ash at 14%.

### p1c2 — Prejudices of Philosophers · "Sphinx over the Shattered Table"
- **Rationale.** The demolition chapter: Sphinx vs Oedipus and the
  "rendezvous of questions and notes of interrogation" (§1); "THE BELIEF IN
  ANTITHESES OF VALUES" (§2); Tartuffe-Kant luring us down "dialectic
  by-ways," Spinoza in "mail and mask" (§5); the Table of Categories and
  "virtus dormitiva" comedy (§11); "pale, cold, grey conceptional networks"
  over "the motley whirl of the senses" (§14); Munchausen hauling himself
  "by the hair, out of the slough of nothingness" (§21); the closing
  sea-voyage "right OVER morality" crowning psychology "queen of the
  sciences" (§23). Art stages the inquisition: the questioner questioned.
- **Palette role.** Brass chapter: interrogation gold. Bone-Black ground,
  Brass linework accents (Sphinx eyes, tablet cracks), Ash hatch. The grey
  conceptual net (§14) appears literally as a fine grey screen-pattern over
  part of the plate — the only chapter allowed a "net" texture.
- **Motif / SVG concept** (`art/front-sphinx-table.svg`, ~1600×900):
  a Sphinx (lion body, woman's head, archaic smile) couchant atop a
  shattered stone table whose fragments bear category-slots (empty headed
  cells: QUANTITY / QUALITY / RELATION / MODALITY, cracked through);
  scattered "?" stones at the base; behind, the §14 grey net (fine diagonal
  cross-hatch veil) half-covering a motley whirl of small sense-emblems
  (eye, ear, hand, tongue — Beardsley-solid blacks); a tiny traveler with a
  staff (Oedipus) at the left edge for scale. Caption: "Which of us is the
  Oedipus here? Which the Sphinx? —§1."
- **Opener composition.** Same chamber grammar; ghost "I"; epigraph:
  "WHY NOT RATHER untruth? And uncertainty? Even ignorance? —§1."
  Below the epigraph, a brass rule set with 23 small ticks (one per § —
  furniture, countable, honest).
- **Reading treatment.** Longest chapter (7430 words, 23 §§): needs stamina
  aids without touching prose. Drop cap on §1; increased space before
  paragraphs that open a § is NOT achievable with plain `<p>`s, so instead:
  running outer-margin vignette (Sphinx paw + "?" mark, alternating
  sides is forbidden — keep outer side only); Latin display lines
  ("Adventavit asinus…," "Quia est in eo virtus dormitiva…") will render as
  ordinary paragraphs — request (not require) of ReaderLead: no special
  handling needed, their brevity already gives rhythm. `::selection`
  oxblood. Optional reader-contract request: wrap leading "N." numerals in
  a `.aph-num` span so numerals can be Brass — design works with or without.

### p1c3 — The Free Spirit · "The Mask in High Air"
- **Rationale.** Masks, altitude, tempo: "have your masks and your ruses"
  and the "garden with golden trellis-work" (§25); the citadel (§26);
  gangasrotogati-presto vs kurmagati-lento vs frog-staccato (§27); TEMPO
  untranslatable, Petronius's "feet of a wind," Aristophanes under Plato's
  pillow (§28); the labyrinth and "minotaur of conscience" (§29); heights
  "from which tragedy itself no longer appears to operate tragically" and
  "PURE air" (§30); "sec, clair, sans illusion" (§39); "Everything that is
  profound loves the mask… like an old, green, heavily-hooped wine-cask"
  (§40); the bird's "voluptuous distance" and "the danger of the flier"
  (§41); "tempters," "the abysses for the profound," "midnight and midday
  solitude" (§§42–44). The treatment must feel drier, higher, faster.
- **Palette role.** Palest chapter: lift the chamber — Plate ground lightened
  toward `#211B14`, more Bone Paper in the plate (snow/air), Brass for the
  trellis lines, a single verdigris-cask green note `#4F6B5A` allowed ONLY
  here (the old green cask, §40) as the chapter's rationed third color.
- **Motif / SVG concept** (`art/front-mask-altitude.svg`, ~1600×900):
  a Venetian bauta mask floating large at center-left, its eye-holes
  showing not eyes but tiny engraved mountain peaks; from the mask's lower
  edge a golden trellis-line (§25) descends and unravels into a labyrinth
  thread (§29) that runs to the lower-right corner where a minotaur horn
  just breaks the frame; upper-right, three birds at increasing altitude
  (the flier, §41), the highest one Brass; background: horizontal wind-hatch
  (Petronius's wind-feet) with a small staccato/legato/presto mark trio
  hidden in the hatch (frog/tortoise/river tempos, §27 — an Easter egg for
  the attentive). Caption: "Everything that is profound loves the mask. —§40."
- **Opener composition.** Ghost "II"; epigraph: "il faut etre sec, clair,
  sans illusion. —§39 (Stendhal)." Airier spacing than p1c2 (padding +25%);
  plate frame here is a double keyline (fine + hairline) to suggest altitude
  instruments.
- **Reading treatment.** Presto passages (§§27–28) set with the tempo rule
  (leading 1.6); the §41 "Not to cleave…" anaphora renders as one long
  paragraph — calm leading returns for it. Margin vignette: small bauta eye.
  Footnote glosses ([Footnote: Like the river Ganges…]) render inline —
  no action; their parenthetical texture suits the chapter.

### p1c4 — The Religious Mood · "The Ladder of Cruelty"
- **Rationale.** The book's blood-warm center: virgin-forest "big hunt"
  (§45); "continuous suicide of reason" and "'God on the Cross'" (§46);
  saint-worship wrecking psychology (§47); Greek "irrestrainable stream of
  GRATITUDE" vs Christian fear (§49); the mighty halting before the saint
  (§51); Old-Testament colossi vs New-Testament "ROCOCO" (§52); and the
  chapter's architectural gift — "a great ladder of religious cruelty, with
  many rounds; but three of these are the most important": firstlings →
  instincts/"nature" → "to sacrifice God himself… To sacrifice God for
  nothingness" (§55); "circulus vitiosus deus" (§56); idleness vs "noisy,
  time-engrossing… laboriousness" (§58); piety as "the most elaborate and
  ultimate product of the FEAR of truth," man made "iridescent" (§59);
  "To REVERSE all estimates of value," "a SUBLIME ABORTION of man," "some
  divine hammer" (§62). This is where the hoarded oxblood is spent.
- **Palette role.** Oxblood chapter — the arc's ember. Ground deepens toward
  `#100C0A` (warmest black), Bone linework, Oxblood rungs/flame, Brass only
  for the halo-foil details. Highest accent saturation in the cohort.
- **Motif / SVG concept** (`art/front-ladder-cruelty.svg`, ~1600×1000,
  tallest plate): a great ladder rising from bottom-center into a dark cloud
  mass at top; its many rungs Ash, its THREE key rungs Oxblood and slightly
  wider, each with a small incised emblem — rung 1: firstling lamb (§55.1),
  rung 2: a knotted instinct-cord/flayed heart (§55.2), rung 3 (topmost,
  half-dissolved into the cloud): an empty throne-back / broken halo
  (§55.3, God-for-nothingness); at the foot, a sacrificial flame (the only
  soft shape in the cohort — everything else is line) with three ascending
  sparks; in the cloud, a faint vicious circle (circulus vitiosus deus,
  §56) and three new stars (§57); far below-left, a tiny hammer (§62)
  lying beside an uncut stone block. Caption: "To sacrifice God for
  nothingness. —§55."
- **Opener composition.** Ghost "III"; epigraph: "There is a great ladder of
  religious cruelty, with many rounds. —§55." Plate taller than siblings
  (ladder needs verticality); frame keyline in Oxblood — the only oxblood
  frame in the cohort.
- **Reading treatment.** Slowest chapter (idleness, §58): leading 1.8,
  widest measure (44rem) for a processional pace; drop cap in Oxblood;
  the French/Latin display fragments (§48 Renan, §53–54) read as
  chant-fragments — surround with extra paragraph spacing via
  `:nth` impossible; instead the margin vignette becomes a thin vertical
  ladder-rule with three oxblood ticks running the chapter's full height
  (background-attachment: local on the chapter container — calm, no motion).
  Most devotional reading surface in the book.

### p1c5 — Apophthegms and Interludes · "Sparks round the Abyss"
- **Rationale.** 100+ maxims, §63–185, 1–3 lines each: "FETTERED HEART, FREE
  SPIRIT" (§87), ice that burns (§91), child's play-seriousness (§94),
  salted truth (§81), poisoned Eros (§168), Cyclops tenderness (§171)… and
  the sentence the whole book is remembered by: "if thou gaze long into an
  abyss, the abyss will also gaze into thee" (§146). Form IS content here:
  chain-lightning, sparks, "joyous distrust" (§154). The opener must be the
  fastest, lightest, most electric surface in the cohort — release after
  p1c4's ember.
- **Palette role.** Spark chapter: near-monochrome Bone on Bone-Black with
  Brass sparks; oxblood ration NOT spent here (restraint after the ladder).
  Highest line-density, smallest color area — steel and starlight.
- **Motif / SVG concept** (`art/front-abyss-sparks.svg`, ~1600×800, widest
  plate): center, the abyss-eye — concentric engraved circles (7 rings,
  alternating solid/hairline) forming a great eye whose pupil is pure void
  (paper-black, no hatch); a tiny figure at the rim leaning over the edge,
  his gaze-line (one hairline) dropping into the pupil while a return
  hairline rises from the pupil to his face (the mutual gaze, §146);
  radiating outward, ~40 fine spark-lines of varying length, twelve of them
  tipped with Brass §-numerals (§§ 68, 71, 81, 87, 91, 94, 146, 150, 153,
  154, 168, 171 — the memorable ones, a finder-chart for re-readers);
  corners: four small emblems — fettered heart (§87), salt cellar (§81),
  peacock feather (§73A "Many a peacock hides his tail"), Cyclops eye
  (§171). Caption: "if thou gaze long into an abyss, the abyss will also
  gaze into thee. —§146."
- **Opener composition.** Ghost "IV"; epigraph: "Objection, evasion, joyous
  distrust, and love of irony are signs of health. —§154." Plate widest and
  shortest (a frieze — the maxim-chain frieze); below it, the titled
  sub-poems ("THE SAGE AS ASTRONOMER" etc.) render as plain paragraphs —
  their caps already give rhythm; do not add rules between maxims (the
  reader's paragraph rhythm IS the chain).
- **Reading treatment.** Presto: 1.0rem body, leading 1.55, measure 38rem
  (narrower = faster saccades); no drop cap (maxims don't take caps —
  restraint); first paragraph (§63, on teachers) opens flush. Margin
  vignette: single spark-asterisk. Titled maxims get no extra styling —
  honesty rule: plain `<p>` rendering is the chain; any numbering spans
  await the optional ReaderLead hook noted in p1c2.

## 5. Mood arc (how the five form one movement)

**Night → Inquisition → High Air → Ember → Sparks.**

1. **p1c1 Preface — the drawn bow (tension).** Darkest, quietest: rubble
   below, stars above, one arrow. Brass only. The reader enters the chamber.
2. **p1c2 Prejudices — the inquisition (demolition).** Longest, most
   argumentative: Sphinx-gold interrogation, grey nets, comedy (ass, clock,
   Munchausen). Brass dominant; the chamber gets crowded with exhibits.
3. **p1c3 Free Spirit — the ascent (altitude).** Palest, driest, fastest:
   masks, wind-hatch, birds, trellis-gold. The chamber opens a window onto
   high air. The cask-green note is the arc's only hue surprise.
4. **p1c4 Religious Mood — the ember (blood-warmth).** Slowest, tallest,
   most saturated: the hoarded oxblood spent on three rungs and one flame.
   Emotional nadir/zenith — cruelty faced directly. The chamber becomes a
   chapel.
5. **p1c5 Interludes — the sparks (release).** Shortest lines, fastest
   surface, near-monochrome: steel starlight after chapel dark. The eye
   closes the movement by gazing back.

Palette narrative: Brass accumulates (p1c1→p1c2), air bleaches (p1c3),
Oxblood ignites once (p1c4), sparks scatter (p1c5). A reader paging
straight through feels one night's weather, not five skins.

TOC treatment (for the engineer + book supervisor): each
`.toc-chapter[data-chapter-id]` row carries a 40px vignette cropped from
its plate (bow-nock / Sphinx eye / mask eye / ladder rung / abyss pupil)
at 70% opacity, Brass on hover. Preface row keeps the Sils Maria dateline
as its description line.
